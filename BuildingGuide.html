Building Your Portfolio

The Building Guide will give you a clear starting point for developing the content that will shape your creative portfolio. Here, we'll figure out which projects to highlight, how to organize your work, and which tools can make your portfolio look polished and professional.

What's a Portfolio?

If you're new to a creative field or still in school, you might be wondering what actually belongs in a portfolio. The good news is that you don't need years of experience or dozens of projects to get started. A portfolio is simply a collection of your work that tangibly highlights your skills, interests, and strengths as a creative. In industries like graphic design, animation, brand marketing, film, videography, and UX/UI, portfolios often matter just as much as the resume. Schools, employers, and clients use them to see the quality of your work, your specific style, and whether you're a good fit for an opportunity in schooling, internships, freelance work, or other roles.

So, let's break down the process of building a portfolio!

Creating Your Content

One of the biggest misconceptions about portfolios is that every project needs to come from a job or internship. In reality, many students build their first portfolios using class assignments, personal projects, and other creative endeavors. If you're still developing your skills, focus on creating work that reflects your interests and the type of career you hope to pursue.

Where do portfolio projects come from?

  • Class Projects - Class assignments are often the easiest place to start. Design projects, IMC work, short films, and websites can all become strong portfolio pieces.
  • Personal Projects - Personal projects let you explore topics and creative styles that interest you. They also show commitment, versitility, and give you to freedom to present your own creative flair.
  • Mockup Client Work - If you don't have real clients yet, create your own project. Designing a logo, brand identity, or marketing campaign for a fictional company can still demonstrate valuable skills.
  • Group Projects and Clubs - Group assignments, student organizations, and creative clubs can provide portfolio-worthy experience. Just be sure to explain your role so viewers understand your contributions.
  • Internships and Early Work - Internships, freelance projects, and entry-level jobs will become valuable additions to your portfolio as you gain experience. Like with group projects, focus on the work that best represents your skillset and clearly describe how you contributed.

Industry Programs

While we'll go into more detail about each platform on Tools and Resources, it's good to become aqauinted with software/programs you'll likely encounter in several creative fields.

Abobe Photoshop

Adobe Photoshop is one of the most widely used creative programs for photo editing, digital artwork, image manipulation, and marketing graphics. It's commonly used in graphic design, photography, IMC/brand marketing, and content creation.

Adobe Illustrator

Adobe Illustrator is used to create logos, vector graphics, icons, typography, and brand assets. It's an industry standard in graphic design and branding

Adobe Premiere Pro

Premiere Pro is a professional video editing program used for films, commercials, social media content, and digital marketing campaigns. It's widely used throughout the film, videography, and content creation spheres, and experience with this program is often expected for intern or entry-level positions.

Adobe After Effects

After Effects specializes in motion graphics, animation, visual effects, and animated text, commonly used in marketing campaigns, adverts, and a variety of animated content.

Figma

Figma is a popular design tool used to create websites, mobile apps, and digital interfaces. It's especially useful for students interested in UX/UI, web design, or digital product design.

Canva

Canva is popular for its intuituve interface and used to create social media graphics, presentations, and marketing materials. It's a great starting point for those interested in communication studies, marketing, and content creation who want to create professional projects without a steep learning curve of other programs.

Toon Boom Harmony

Toon Boom Harmony is one of the most widely used programs in 2D animation. Creatives interested in animation can use it to create character animations, compositions, and character rigs while learning workflows similar to those used in professional studios.

Maya Autodesk

Maya is a leading software for 3D modeling and animation in film, television, and game development. Creatives pursuing animation, visual effects, or game design often learn Maya to create character models, environments, and animations used in professional production pipelines.

Don't have access to professional software yet? Many students start with free alternatives such as Blender, DaVinci Resolve, Photopea, Inkscape, and Audacity. While industry-standard tools are worth learning, strong portfolio projects matter more than the software used to create them.

Choosing Your Best Projects

Not every project you've completed needs to be included in your portfolio. The goal isn't to show everything you've ever made, but to intentionally select the work that best represents your skills, interests, and goals.

Consider your goals

Before selecting projects, think about the kind of opportunities you're hoping to pursue. Different creative fields often look for different skills, which means the projects you choose should support the direction you want to take.

For example...

  • Graphic designers may focus on branding, advertising, typography, and visual identity work.
  • Animators may prioritize character animation, storyboards, and motion studies.
  • Videographers may focus on editing projects, cinematography, and promotional content.

Choosing projects with your goals in mind help your portfolio that feels focused on scope.

What makes a project worth including?

A project doesn't need to be perfect to earn a spot in your portfolio, but it should demonstrate something about your abilities as a creative. When reviewing your work, ask yourself: Does this project reflect my current skill level? Did I learn something meaningful from it? Can I explain the creative decisions I made? Does it demonstrate my relevant skillset?

Projects that meet these criteria are strong candidates for your portfolio.

Show variety in Your field

Many portfolio guides encourage students to show variety, but that doesn't mean including completely unrelated work. Instead of cramming in a little bit of everything, focus on demonstrating different skills within your area of interest. ex: A portfolio for a videographer might include a short documentary, a promotional video, a social media campaign, and an editing reel.

How Many Projects Do You Need?

You don't need a dozen or more projects to show your proficiency of a certain skill. A smaller collection of purposful, well-developed work is often easier to review and creates a stronger impression.

Presentation

Once you've selected your strongest projects, the next step is preparing them for presentation. A strong portfolio collection should be easy to understand, organized, and focused on the key takeaways for your employers or clients too remember.

Arranging Projects

Group related projects together

One of the easiest ways to improve a creative portfolio is by organizing similar projects together. Grouping related work helps visitors quickly recognize your areas of expertise and makes your portfolio easier to navigate.

For example, a graphic design portfolio might include sections for branding, advertising, social media graphics and web/UX design, while an animation portfolio might include sections for character animation, storyboards, and motion graphics.

Lead with your strongest work

Realistically, many employers, instructors, and clients review a large number of portfolios and won't view every project in detail, so your strongest work should appear near the beginning of your portfolio. Rather than organizing projects by date or placing your favorite piece at the end, start with the project that best represents your current abilities and interests.

Presenting individual Projects

Showcase Process Strategically

Many fields value process because it provides insight into how ideas develop through each stage and gives viewers a glimpse into your workflow. You don't need to include every draft and change, just a couple of pieces to help viewers better understand your creative thinking and decision-making.

Let's go back to our examples:

  • Graphic desingers may include concept sketches and moodboards.
  • Animators may have reference sheets, storyboards, and rough tests.
  • Videographers may use storyboards and other production planning materials.
  • IMC portfolios may include research and campaign materials.

State Your Contributions

Most portfolio projects on a larger scale are team based, with several people contributing with their specific skillsets. When including team projects, make sure viewers can easily identify your role. This further helps employers understand your skillset and how you perform in team-based assingments.

Consistency is Key!

Consistency can make a portfolio feel more organized and professional. Consider maintaining uniform image layout, clear project titles and descriptions, cohesive theming/design, consistent typography/colors, and easy navigation around your portfolio

The goal of presentation is not to overwhelm viewers with information. Instead, focus on organizing projects thoughtfully, highlighting your strongest work, and presenting each project in a clear way.

Choosing Your Platform

Once your projects are selected and organized, you'll need a platform to share your work. The best choice depends on your field, goals, and how much customization you want.

What should you consider?

  • Your Field - Different platforms appeal to different fields. Consider where professionals in your area of interest typically share their work and whether that platform supports the kind of projects you want to showcase.
  • Usability and Customization - Some platforms are designed for simplicity, while others offer greater creative control. Think about how much customization you need and how much time you're willing to spend learning the tools or building your portfolio.
  • Cost - As a student or emerging creative, you don't need to spend a lot on a portfolio right out the gate. Many platforms start on free or low-cost platforms and grow alongside your skills and experience, so choose the platform that best fits your current budget.

Popular Platforms

Adobe Portfolio helps students and creatives create simple portfolio websites with minimal setup, especially useful for users already working within the Adobe ecosystem.

Artstation

ArtStation is commonly used by animators, game developers, concept artists, and 3D artists. It's visually-focused design allows for easy implementation of process work and digital projects.

Wix

Wix is a website builder that offers greater customization than many portfolio-specific platforms. It's a popular choice for creatives who want more advanced control over their website's design.

Squarespace

Squarespace is popular for its polished templates, presentation and beginner-friendly interface, frequently used by photographers, artists, and other visual workers.
